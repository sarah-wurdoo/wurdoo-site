The Craft

What is a Wurdoo?

A rope that once carried stones across the plateau now carries a wish: hold your own future in your own hand.

For millennia, Tibetan nomads have braided the uddum (乌朵) from hand-spun yak wool — a sling with a woven cradle at its center, whirled overhead to guide herds across the open range. Every nomad once carried one. Each family wove its own.

The craft survives in the hands of a few. In Maozha Township, Yushu, the herding women still knot the traditional braids — and now, with us, they weave new life into them: belts, phone chains, amulet bracelets.

The Artisans

Woven before the digging season

挖虫草季之前，她们编好了这些

Maozha Township · Yushu · Qinghai

Our pieces are knotted by herding women of Maozha Township — in their homes, between milking and herding, before they leave for the caterpillar-fungus season each spring.

When we receive a new batch, it comes with the weather report of the grasslands: snow in June, a hard year for digging — and seventeen bracelets that carry the quiet of those weeks.

This is not a factory rhythm. It is the pace of a pastoral year, and we protect it: made-to-order, small batches, fair returns to the makers.

From a Schoolhouse to a Workshop

It began with a question: what if help didn't stop at donated coats?

01

A Teaching Post in the Grasslands

Years ago we taught at a boarding school in Xialaxiu, Yushu — 80 children aged 4 to 16, and a care home next door.

02

Winters, Then School Years

Coat drives each season grew into one-to-one schooling sponsorships that continue today.

03

From Giving to Making

We asked the headmaster what local craft the community had. He sent photos of handwoven slings. One look — and Wurdoo began.

04

Income, Dignity, Craft

Now the women of Maozha earn from what their hands already knew. The weave that guarded herds now carries their work to the world.
