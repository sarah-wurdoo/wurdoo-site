The herder's sling

The uddum (乌朵) is the Tibetan herder's sling — a long braid of hand-spun yak wool with a woven cradle, the wuti (乌梯), at its center. A stone sits in the cradle; the sling whirls overhead; the stone flies a hundred meters to turn the herd, or to startle a wolf from the flock.

Descended from the ancient shepherd's sling carried across Europe, Asia and the Andes, the Tibetan uddum is its most refined living form: not just a tool, but a woven object of pride, patterned by its maker and passed down through generations.

How it's made

Yak wool is teased and spun by hand into coarse yarn, then braided in traditional patterns — the women of Maozha Township count three classic braids. The sling closes with a loop for the finger at one end and a wool lash at the other. A full piece takes days of knotting.

Why it matters now

As pastoral life changes, fewer young hands learn the braid. Weaving for the world gives the craft a reason to continue — and gives you an object with a real maker, a real season, and a real story behind every knot.

九

The Number Nine

In Tibetan culture, nine is the number of completeness — the fullness of attainment. The Nine-Eye braid gathers that meaning into its woven eyes.

眼

The Eye

The eye sees through darkness — wisdom and light against misfortune. Nine eyes on a woven band: a quiet amulet for modern days.

牧

The Nomad Spirit

A free heart is nomadic. The wurdoo traveled with herders across the plateau; now it travels with you — city streets, long roads, anywhere.
